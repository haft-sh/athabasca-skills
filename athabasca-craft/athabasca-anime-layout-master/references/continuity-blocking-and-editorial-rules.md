# Continuity, Blocking, and Editorial Rules

## Purpose

This reference supplements the anime-layout skill with practical cinematographic and editorial continuity rules so shots do not merely look strong in isolation, but also **cut together cleanly into a finished scene**.

It synthesizes recurring professional guidance from continuity-editing, screen-direction, eyeline, blocking, coverage, and editorial practice.

## Source threads consulted
- StudioBinder: continuity editing, screen direction, eyeline match, Walter Murch's Rule of Six
- Sony Cine / Steven Bernstein ASC: scene blocking, complementary shots, axis, coverage strategy
- Common professional continuity practice reflected in script supervision and coverage workflows

## Core principle
A good shot is not only strong as a frame. It must also preserve:
1. **Geography** — where everyone is
2. **Direction** — where they move and look
3. **Action continuity** — what exactly the body is doing
4. **State continuity** — props, costume, wounds, light, weather, time, energy level
5. **Editorial motivation** — why the cut happens here and what the viewer is meant to track

If a shot cannot cut to the previous and next shot without confusion, it is incomplete.

## Blocking principles

### 1) Blocking must express dramatic power
Blocking is not traffic management. It should clarify:
- who dominates
- who yields
- who invades whose space
- who is isolated
- whether the scene opens up, compresses, or reverses power

### 2) Keep body logic consistent
Track for each beat:
- standing / sitting / kneeling
- body angle to camera
- head turn direction
- hand usage
- prop hand
- step count / stride phase when cutting on movement
- whether the subject is advancing, retreating, circling, or holding ground

### 3) Respect spatial anchors
Use doors, tables, windows, vehicles, staircases, pillars, horizon lines, and strong background landmarks as anchors so the audience can re-read space across cuts.

### 4) Don’t flatten the scene into disconnected singles
Before isolating faces, establish:
- the line of action
- who faces whom
- distance between players
- where exits / threats / objectives are

### 5) Blocking changes must be motivated
If a character crosses the room, sits, turns away, or closes distance, it should correspond to a change in:
- emotional temperature
- dominance
- revelation
- intention
- threat

## Editorial continuity rules

### 1) The 180-degree rule / line of action
Establish an axis through the interacting subjects or movement path.
- Keep the camera on one side to preserve left/right relationships.
- Cross intentionally only when:
  - the audience sees the camera move across the axis,
  - a neutral / axis shot resets geography,
  - or disorientation is dramatically intended.

### 2) Screen direction
Maintain directional flow.
- If a subject exits frame right, the next shot usually continues the movement coherently.
- If a character advances screen left-to-right, preserve that vector unless the story requires resistance or reversal.
- In action, a broken vector creates mush immediately.

### 3) Eyeline continuity
Match not only left/right but also **height** and **distance**.
Check:
- is the subject looking screen left or right?
- slightly up, level, or down?
- at another character, an object, or off-screen space?
- does the reverse shot return the corresponding angle?

### 4) Match on action
Hide cuts inside movement.
Good cut points include:
- sitting / standing
- turning the head
- opening a door
- raising a weapon
- stepping through a threshold
- grabbing / releasing an object

The action shape, speed, and direction must match closely enough that the eye accepts one continuous gesture.

### 5) The 30-degree rule
When cutting between two shots of the same subject, change angle enough to avoid a weak near-duplicate.
- Too small a change produces an accidental jump cut.
- If you cannot change angle enough, change size, lens logic, or framing purpose.

### 6) Eye trace
The important point in frame B should not force the audience to hunt unnecessarily after frame A.
Guide the cut by preserving or deliberately redirecting the viewer’s eye path.

### 7) Cut on intention, not only on motion
Movement can hide a cut, but the stronger criterion is often the dramatic beat:
- when the thought lands
- when power changes hands
- when attention shifts
- when a reaction becomes more important than the action itself

This aligns with Walter Murch’s hierarchy: emotion and story outrank strict spatial purity.

## Coverage rules for editability

### 1) Know the editorial job of each setup
Each shot should have a purpose such as:
- establish geography
- track a power shift
- isolate a reaction
- clarify a gesture or prop
- provide an insert or cutaway for compression or repair
- bridge a line cross or continuity cheat

### 2) Essential coverage types
Depending on scene complexity, useful coverage often includes:
- establishing / master or equivalent geography shot
- medium coverage for interaction and body logic
- singles for performance emphasis
- inserts for hands, objects, mechanisms, glances, feet, doors, triggers
- cutaways / environmental buffers for restructuring timing
- neutral or axial shot when a line transition may be needed

### 3) Complementary reverses
Reverse angles should feel like they belong to the same scene language.
Keep an intentional relationship in:
- focal length / perspective family
- horizon level
- camera height
- intensity of lens-to-eyeline relationship
- movement style (static vs moving)
- lighting logic and balance

### 4) Multi-character scenes need hierarchy
In 3+ character scenes, don’t rely only on alternating singles.
Preserve:
- who is talking to whom
- who watches silently
- who is excluded from the conversational triangle
- whether the group geometry changes

## Continuity categories to track explicitly

### Spatial continuity
- left/right relationships
- distance between subjects
- entrances and exits
- foreground/background relations
- environment orientation

### Performance continuity
- emotional intensity
- tempo of delivery
- pause length
- gaze timing
- hand timing
- breath / posture state

### Prop and costume continuity
- which hand holds the object
- prop orientation / level / depletion state
- coat open or closed
- sleeves rolled or not
- hat / hair / blood / dirt / tears consistency

### Lighting and environmental continuity
- key light direction
- shadow side
- weather level
- smoke / haze density
- practicals on/off
- time-of-day consistency

### Motion continuity
- run direction
- speed
- acceleration / deceleration
- whether motion is completed in frame or interrupted by the cut

## When to break continuity on purpose
Break rules deliberately only when the effect is worth the cost.
Good reasons include:
- subjective panic or madness
- surreal rupture
- moral / psychological inversion
- sudden change of dominance
- dream logic
- comic shock

If breaking continuity intentionally, make the break legible as a choice rather than a mistake.

## Practical shot-design checklist
Before approving a shot, ask:
1. What is the axis?
2. What is the screen direction?
3. What is the eyeline height and side?
4. What body action must match into or out of this shot?
5. What prop / hand / costume state must be preserved?
6. What previous shot can cut into this, and what next shot can cut out of it?
7. Does this shot add new editorial value, or is it redundant coverage?
8. If the line is crossed, how is geography re-established?
9. If the shot is beautiful but difficult to cut, is there support coverage?
10. Is the audience tracking emotion first, then story, then rhythm, then eye trace, then spatial clarity?

## Editorial failure patterns to watch for
- characters appear to look the same direction in dialogue
- a subject teleports across the room between cuts
- a prop changes hands or position
- action restarts or double-finishes after the cut
- cut points land after the dramatic beat instead of on it
- variants change composition but forget continuity bridges
- all shots are individually strong but none provide repair coverage
- the scene has mood but no geographic legibility

## How this should affect anime-layout responses
When designing or analyzing shots, include continuity implications:
- identify axis and screen direction
- note whether the setup is compatible with likely reverses
- call out blocking risks if the body turn, hand choice, or movement vector may fail in editorial assembly
- propose inserts / cutaways / neutral shots when a scene likely needs editorial glue
- when offering variants, explain how each version changes not only emotion but also editability
