# Story Structure and Dramatic Engine

## Core principle

A screenplay is not a sequence of events. It is a designed pressure system. A protagonist wants something, the world resists, and the conflict forces choices that reveal and change character.

## Primary diagnostic model

Use this chain:

**Balance → Inciting Incident → Desire → Opposition → Escalation → Crisis Choice → Climax → New Balance**

If any link is missing, the story will feel soft, confusing, episodic, or unfinished.

## Dramatic engine checklist

- Who is the protagonist?
- What is their external want?
- What is their internal need?
- What is their flawed strategy or lie?
- What event disrupts balance?
- Why can’t they simply ignore the problem?
- What opposes them?
- How does opposition escalate?
- What choice do they make under pressure?
- How does the ending show change?

## Want / Need / Flaw

### Want
The visible goal. The audience should be able to say what the protagonist is trying to do.

### Need
The deeper lesson, acceptance, emotional skill, or moral truth. This should not be delivered as a lecture; it should be forced by events.

### Flaw
The habit that blocks the character. Good flaws are active strategies, not vague labels.

Weak: “She is insecure.”
Strong: “She overprepares so she never has to ask for help.”

## Antagonism

Antagonism is any force that makes the protagonist’s desire harder to achieve:

- Inner: fear, pride, shame, impatience, burnout, denial
- Personal: rival, parent, friend, coach, teammate, sibling
- Social: school pressure, team expectations, status, rules
- Environmental: storm, deadline, distance, physical limitation
- Cosmic / thematic: mortality, fate, time, nature, growing up

Strong stories layer antagonism. The outer obstacle should activate the inner flaw.

## Escalation

Escalation means each attempt changes the situation and makes the next attempt harder.

Weak escalation: same obstacle repeats.
Strong escalation: each failure costs something, reveals something, or narrows options.

## Crisis choice

A crisis choice should force the protagonist to choose between:

- the external want and the internal need
- the old coping strategy and a new one
- selfish success and relational/moral repair
- safety and growth
- control and trust

## Ending

A strong ending answers the opening pressure with a changed image or action.

Ask:

- Does the ending resolve the dramatic question?
- Does it show the protagonist acting differently?
- Is the theme visible through behavior?
- Does the final image contrast with the opening image?

## Structure models to use as tools

### Three-act logic

- Act 1: setup and disruption
- Act 2: pursuit and complication
- Act 3: choice and consequence

### Mythic compression for shorts

- Ordinary state
- Disruption
- Resistance
- Trial
- Insight
- New action
- Return changed

### Moral-argument structure

- Weakness hurts self or others
- Desire exposes weakness
- Opponent attacks weakness
- Crisis forces moral choice
- Self-revelation changes behavior
- Ending proves the moral action
