# Short-Form Pattern Library

Patterns are starting points, not recipes. Borrow the mechanic, then make the premise, copy, art direction, product role, and payoff original.

## Core content engines

| Engine | Viewer desire | Hook shape | Hold mechanism | Natural product role |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Challenge / test | Prove competence or identity | “Can you get 5/5?” | escalating difficulty | place to play the full version |
| Transformation | See change | show result first | reveal process/steps | tool that causes the result |
| Comparison / ranking | Resolve a choice | “Which is actually better?” | criteria revealed sequentially | source of data or evaluator |
| Useful shortcut | Save time/money/friction | name costly problem | stepwise method | tool that implements shortcut |
| Myth vs evidence | Correct belief | surprising contradiction | proof ladder | source/method behind proof |
| Serial mystery | Resolve uncertainty | concrete anomaly | clues and elimination | environment containing more cases |
| Experiment | Discover outcome | visible question/test | progress and uncertainty | instrument or subject of test |
| Process / behind scenes | Access hidden craft | result + “here’s how” | decisions and mistakes | workflow/product in authentic use |
| Reaction / commentary | Borrow context, add judgment | strongest moment first | interpretation/escalation | expert lens or community home |
| Participation / remix | Be seen and compare | simple creative constraint | submissions/replies | generator, template, or destination |
| Story / reveal | Feel change or surprise | conflict at the point of change | causality and stakes | enabling solution, not ad break |
| Spectacle / craft | Experience awe or delight | impossible-looking image | scale/detail progression | means of creation or access |

## Challenge design

A good challenge needs:

1. **Instant rules** — understandable in one glance.
2. **Attemptability** — difficult enough to matter, possible enough to try.
3. **Progress visibility** — numbered rounds, timer, score, lives, or levels.
4. **Escalation** — rounds change in difficulty or type.
5. **Resolution** — answers and score interpretation.
6. **Comparison** — viewers can compare with friends or a community.
7. **Continuation** — a natural harder/next edition.

### Weak challenge

> “Guess these five things. Comment below.”

Problems: generic status, interchangeable rounds, high comment effort, no revealed answer, no reason to follow.

### Stronger challenge skeleton

- **Opening:** “Football shirt test: 4/5 means your ball knowledge is elite.”
- **Round 1:** recognizable silhouette; 2 seconds.
- **Round 2:** color/history clue removed.
- **Round 3:** rivalry misdirection.
- **Round 4:** obscure but fair contextual clue.
- **Round 5:** “impossible” bonus.
- **Reveal:** answers + score tiers.
- **Continuation:** “Tomorrow: national teams, impossible mode.”
- **Product bridge:** “Play the full daily board at [destination].”

The specific subject and copy must be rebuilt for the account.

## Carousel / photo-post grammar

Each card has one job.

1. **Card 1 — contract:** topic, challenge/value, and reason to swipe.
2. **Card 2 — immediate proof:** reward the first swipe quickly.
3. **Middle cards — progression:** escalate, vary, compare, or reveal.
4. **Penultimate card — peak:** hardest, most surprising, or most useful beat.
5. **Final card — payoff:** answer, score, summary, or transformation.
6. **CTA layer — continuation:** place after or inside the payoff, not instead of it.

For a five-card challenge, using all five cards as unanswered questions often creates no payoff. Better options:

- 1 hook + 3 questions + 1 answers/score card,
- alternating question/answer cards,
- or convert stills into video with timed reveals.

## Short video grammar

A compact structure:

1. **0–1 sec:** visual/topic recognition and tension.
2. **1–3 sec:** rules/value contract.
3. **Body:** additive beats; no repeated setup.
4. **Peak:** hardest, strangest, most useful, or most emotional moment.
5. **Payoff:** answer/proof/result.
6. **Close:** one proportionate next action.
7. **Loop:** only if it feels natural, not deceptive.

Time ranges are heuristics, not laws. Let comprehension and real retention data decide.

## Hook families

### Specific curiosity

- “One detail gives this away.”
- “Most people change their answer on round three.”
- “This looks like X. It is actually Y.”

The missing information must feel both important and realistically knowable.

### Identity and status

- “Only [specific community] notices number four.”
- “Your score reveals what kind of [identity] you are.”

Avoid insulting viewers or making impossible claims.

### Utility

- “Before you pay for X, check this.”
- “The 10-second fix for [specific frustration].”

Supply proof quickly.

### Visible conflict

- “We tried the advice everyone repeats. It failed.”
- “Two tools, one task, no editing tricks.”

Make the comparison fair.

### Transformation-first

Show the outcome before the explanation. The result itself must be legible and desirable.

## Share-design prompts

Before production, complete at least one sentence:

- “Someone sends this to a friend because ______.”
- “Someone saves this because they will need it when ______.”
- “Posting this to a group chat lets the sender signal ______.”
- “Two viewers will disagree meaningfully about ______.”
- “A recipient can participate by ______.”

If every blank is vague (“because it’s cool”), the spread mechanism is underdesigned.

## Product integration patterns

### Product as playground

The feed post gives a sample; the destination offers more rounds, depth, control, or personalization.

### Product as proof

The post makes a claim, then demonstrates the result inside the product.

### Product as tool

The creator uses the product naturally to create the valuable outcome.

### Product as dataset

The product’s unique data powers comparisons, rankings, maps, or insights.

### Product as community

The post starts a conversation; the product is where persistent participation occurs.

Avoid “entertainment, then unrelated logo.” The bridge should preserve the same motivation.

## Repeatable series systems

A series needs a **stable container and variable payload**.

Stable container:

- title/visual grammar,
- audience promise,
- scoring system,
- cadence,
- host attitude,
- or recurring constraint.

Variable payload:

- topic,
- difficulty,
- guest,
- region,
- category,
- tool,
- era,
- submission,
- or consequence.

Ways to delay fatigue:

- easy / hard / impossible,
- themed days,
- viewer-submitted editions,
- head-to-head duets,
- regional or historical editions,
- speed rounds,
- expert versus casual,
- “spot the lie,”
- results/reaction follow-ups,
- and seasonal events.

## Left-field ideation prompts

When concepts feel generic, ask:

- What if the audience controls tomorrow’s post?
- What if the promoted object is never named until the reveal?
- What if the product’s failure is more entertaining than its success?
- What if the format is a game show, courtroom, draft, auction, autopsy, confession, or field test?
- What if the comments become source material for the next episode?
- What could only this account truthfully show?
- What belief does the target audience enjoy proving wrong?
- What is the smallest complete experience the product can give away in-feed?

## Common failure patterns

### Generic engagement bait

“Comment your answer” is not a mechanism. Make the act of answering enjoyable, status-bearing, or socially comparative.

### No payoff

Five questions without answers may earn comments but damage trust and reduce saves/shares. Reveal in-post or promise a specific, timely follow-up only when the delayed reveal is the experience.

### Repetitive carousel

Cards change images but not stakes, difficulty, or knowledge. Add progression.

### Product hidden too well

A post can go broad while teaching viewers nothing about the offer. Integrate the product into the experience, not just the end card.

### Product shown too early

Brand-first framing can read as an ad before value is established. Lead with viewer value while allowing subtle product presence.

### Broad trend, wrong audience

A trend can inflate views among people who will never follow or convert. Rebuild the trend around the target audience’s identity or problem.

### Overproduction

Pristine motion and cinematic polish can reduce native texture or production cadence. Spend polish where it improves comprehension, proof, or distinctiveness.

### Copying execution

A public meme format is reusable; a creator’s distinctive script, sequence, visuals, phrases, jokes, or branded device are not. Transform the mechanic substantially.
